About Me

I’m Alex Kargin, a Senior Data and Analytics Engineer based in Boca Raton, Florida. I have built and supported production data systems since 2013—currently as a contract Data Engineer at Silicon Army, previously at Restaurant Brands International / Burger King and, before that, nearly six years at Orangetheory Fitness.

A recurring thread through that work is migration and modernization. At Orangetheory I led the move from Aurora and Redshift to Snowflake—source-system analysis, schema and data mapping, transformation logic, cleansing, reconciliation, validation, and cutover. Earlier database engineering roles covered migrations across MySQL, PostgreSQL, SQL Server, and Aurora, along with query tuning, OLTP-to-OLAP modeling, and production support through DEV/SIT/UAT/Production release workflows.

I build data solutions end to end—from raw operational data and complicated business rules to trusted Snowflake models, Power BI dashboards, APIs, and production AI applications.

A lot of that modeling is what the industry now calls the semantic layer: defining metrics and business logic once, in the warehouse, so dashboards, applications, and AI agents all read the same numbers instead of each re-deriving them from raw source systems. It is the same dimensional modeling and dbt work I have been doing for years, pointed at a new consumer—something I wrote about recently after dbt Labs reported cutting AI token costs ~98% by feeding agents modeled summaries rather than raw source data.

My experience combines data engineering, analytics engineering, business intelligence, and applied AI. I work across Snowflake, dbt, Dagster, Airflow, Power BI, Python, SQL, AWS, Azure, PostgreSQL, ClickHouse, FastAPI, and Next.js.

I have delivered analytics solutions supporting Finance, Accounting, Operations, Marketing, forecasting, and executive reporting. My work includes dimensional modeling, semantic models, DAX, KPI development, data-quality validation, cloud migrations, performance optimization, governance, and production support.

I also build practical AI systems, including document intelligence, RAG, vector search, NLP, computer vision, and private LLM applications. My focus is not AI for demonstration purposes—it is using the right technology to solve a real business problem reliably, securely, and cost-effectively.

FranchiseLens

Document-intelligence engine over public Franchise Disclosure Documents — the ~300-page filings franchisors must register with US states. It pulls filings from state registries and runs a local-LLM extraction pipeline that lifts the numbers buyers actually care about: total initial investment, franchise fee, royalty, ad fund, net-worth and liquid-capital requirements, agreement term, and outlet counts. 908 franchises loaded, with a coverage survey across 65 fitness & sports brands.

Every figure is grounded to the exact page it came from (p.18, p.47…) and flagged for review when it can't be verified — so a side-by-side brand comparison, a natural-language Ask over the documents, and a browsable directory all stay auditable. Runs entirely on self-hosted models, no third-party LLM API.

ClaimLens

Multimodal case-file builder for Florida no-fault (PIP) car-accident claims. It ingests police reports, medical records and itemized bills, insurance declarations, and crash photos, then fills a typed demand-package schema — parties, liability, injuries, damages, lost income, coverage — instead of returning a pile of RAG Q&A. A vision model reads damage and injury photographs into structured findings, not just the text documents.

A chronology engine assembles dated events into one timeline and flags Florida's hard statutory gates — the 14-day PIP treatment rule and the 30-day insurer window — while a checklist engine detects missing documents (radiology, lost-wage proof, liens) before the package is sent. Every conclusion links back to its source page or photo, and the whole package exports as a cited PDF. Demo on synthetic data, running on free local models.

BookkeeperLens

Bookkeeping cleanup engine that turns messy bank statements and a shoebox of receipts into double-entry, Schedule C-ready books — in five stages: upload (PDF/CSV statements plus receipt photos, no bank linking), extract (every statement line becomes a transaction that keeps its page and line provenance; a vision model reads receipts into vendor, date, tax and total), categorize (a Schedule C account with a confidence score and the reason it was chosen, with likely-personal spend flagged instead of deducted), reconcile (each month checked against the statement's ending balance, each receipt matched to the bank line that paid it), and report.

Output is a tax-readiness score out of 100, a prioritized fix list, a P&L and balance sheet, and a trial balance that foots to zero — catching duplicates, miscategorizations, stale uncleared items and missing receipts along the way, with every number traceable to the paper it came from. Built as a self-serve path off outsourced bookkeeping (Bench), running on the free NVIDIA NIM tier or fully private via Ollama.
