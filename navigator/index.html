The question underneath the questions

Navigator is already positioned as decision support rather than reporting — “guides proactive decisions,” “illuminate the unknown.” The product promise is already tell me what I should do. Today a customer gets there by reading a dashboard and interpreting it themselves.

An AI layer closes that last gap: the user asks in their own words and gets a grounded answer with the evidence attached.

But not every question is the same shape — and the most common failure in a product like this is treating all of them as document retrieval.

“How many units of this part are in the Dallas FSL” is a number in a table. Similarity search is the wrong mechanism for an authoritative operational number, and the answer that comes back will be fluent and confident anyway. “What are we contractually obligated to in Brazil” is buried in a 60-page agreement and genuinely needs retrieval. “Expedite this shipment” isn’t a question at all — it’s an action with consequences.

So the first design decision isn’t which model or which vector store. It’s routing.

Proposed shape: route first, then retrieve

Who is asking, what are they allowed to see, and which of the three shapes is this?
Lane 01 · Structured Governed query over operational data

“Do we have this part within four hours of this site?”

Inventory positions, shipment events, SLA clocks. Answered by a governed query or service API against approved metrics — not by similarity search. Whatever Navigator already uses for this is the right place for it to live.

Lane 02 · Documents Grounded retrieval over documents

“What did we do the last three times this failed?”

Contracts and SLA terms, customs and trade rules, field procedures, and case history sitting in free-text notes. Hybrid keyword and vector, reranked, every claim cited to its source. Likely implementation: Azure AI Search with metadata filters, scoring profiles and reranking — assuming that’s the existing platform direction.

Lane 03 · Actions Agents that change something

“Reroute this from the Atlanta stocking location.”

Typed tools against ERP, ticketing and WMS. Read-only tools start at lower operational risk. Write tools are designed to be idempotent, so a retry doesn’t create a duplicate action.

HIGH-IMPACT WRITES GATED · LOW-RISK ACTIONS POLICY-CONTROLLED
Applies to every lane Entitlement is enforced at retrieval, not at display. In a multi-tenant product, one OEM surfacing another’s data is not a bug to patch later.
Applies to every lane Grounding and citation are the interface. A cited answer is how the user decides whether to trust it — and how we debug it when they don’t.

Many real questions cross lanes. “Why are we trending toward an SLA miss on this account?” needs the numbers from Lane 01, the contract terms and exception notes from Lane 02, and possibly an action from Lane 03. That composite question is the most valuable thing Navigator could answer — and the last thing I’d build, not the first.

Where I’d start, and what I’d hold back

Sequenced on two axes: value, and feasibility — where feasibility mostly means is the data ready, and is being wrong safe?

The instinct is to start with the most impressive use case. That one usually has the worst data and the highest cost of being wrong, and a visible failure early can damage trust in the whole program. I’d rather earn the right to build it.

First 90 days

Three phases. The first is shorter than people expect, and the second produces something real in production rather than a prototype.

01Weeks 1–3

Listen, and find out what the data can actually support

Sit with the Navigator product owner and with whoever fields customer questions today — support tickets and account-team email are a free dataset of the exact questions this has to answer. In parallel, meet ops, finance and HR to collect candidate internal use cases.

Then profile the data: what exists across the four service lines, how current it is, and what the entitlement model looks like. Most AI programs slip on data readiness, not on models — I’d rather know that in week two than in month three.

Out: a corrected version of the grid above, and a written read on data readiness per use case.
02Weeks 4–8

One thin slice, in production, with evaluation behind it

Not a demo. Something narrow and real — one document type or one question class — with retrieval, grounding, citations and an evaluation harness. Likely a Lane 02 use case, internal and read-only. How quickly that reaches production depends on your release process and security review, which is one of the things I’d want to understand in week one.

The evaluation set gets built before the pipeline scales, measuring retrieval and generation separately — so when an answer fails we can tell whether it was retrieval, context assembly, source data, or generation. Scoring only the final answer means spending a month tuning prompts to fix what turns out to be a chunking problem.

Out: a working slice people can react to, plus a golden set and a baseline number to improve against.
03Weeks 9–13

Roadmap delivered, phase two underway

By this point the roadmap is grounded in something we built rather than something we imagined — sequenced by value against feasibility, with data prerequisites named per item and honest about what isn’t possible yet.

Alongside it, the reusable pieces: retrieval patterns, the entitlement model, the evaluation harness, agent tooling. The goal is that the second and third use case take weeks, not another project.

Out: the Navigator AI roadmap, and phase two scoped and started.

What I’d need to ask you

Everything above changes depending on the answers to these. They’re the reason this is a draft.

  1. Is the Navigator AI capability aimed at your customers directly, or at Flash teams serving those customers first?

    This changes almost every design decision — guardrail strictness, citation requirements, latency budget, and how much of the entitlement model has to be right on day one.

  2. How consolidated is the data behind Navigator today across ServiceAxis, TradeAssure, FieldReady and ReverseMax?

    Determines whether the first 90 days is retrieval work or data work. Both are fine — but they produce very different roadmaps.

  3. What does the entitlement model look like across customers?

    For a multi-tenant product this is the hardest constraint, and it is far cheaper to design for than to retrofit.

  4. Has anything been built already — a pilot, a proof of concept, a vendor evaluation? What did it teach you?

    Prior attempts usually encode real constraints that aren’t written down anywhere.

  5. Is this a differentiator you intend to sell on, or an internal efficiency play — or both, in sequence?

    Sets whether the first milestone is something a customer sees or something that saves an ops team an afternoon.

  6. Competitively, is the comparison other logistics providers — or the service-parts software vendors?

    Navigator’s AI layer seems to compete less with carriers than with platforms like Syncron or Servigistics. The difference being that you run the operations, so you already own the data those platforms have to be integrated to receive.

  7. You’ve described success as leading Flash in the right direction on agents. What would the wrong direction look like to you?

    The failure you’re trying to avoid tells me more about the job than the outcome you’re aiming at.
